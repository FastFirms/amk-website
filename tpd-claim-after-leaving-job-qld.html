You stopped working months ago, maybe years ago. Your back gave out, your mental health hit a wall, or a medical condition made your job impossible. You've been getting by, and you've probably never looked closely at your super statement.

Here's something a lot of people don't realise: you may have insurance inside your super that could pay a lump sum if you can't work again. And leaving your job doesn't necessarily mean you've lost it. As TPD claims lawyers in Queensland, we often speak with people who had no idea this cover existed. This guide explains how it works.

This article is for Queensland readers. TPD cover in super is governed by Commonwealth superannuation law and the terms of each fund's insurance policy, which vary.

What is TPD insurance?

TPD stands for total and permanent disability. It's insurance that may pay you a lump sum if illness or injury means you're unlikely to ever work again in a job you're suited to. Many Australians have TPD cover automatically through their superannuation fund without realising it.

TPD cover isn't about how you got hurt. It doesn't matter whether it happened at work, in a car accident, playing sport or through illness. It also doesn't need anyone to be at fault. What matters is whether you meet the policy's definition of TPD.

That definition differs between funds and policies. A common version asks whether you're unlikely ever to work again in any job you're reasonably suited to by your education, training or experience. Some policies use different tests, especially for people who weren't working when they became disabled.

Can I claim TPD if I've already left my job?

Often, yes. What usually matters is whether you were covered by the policy at the relevant time, which is often when you stopped work because of your condition. You may still be able to claim even if you left that job, or closed or rolled over that super account, some time ago.

That's the key point people miss. Your claim generally looks back to when you became disabled, not whether you have cover today.

For example, say you worked in a warehouse near Redbank and stopped because of a back injury three years ago. If you had TPD cover through your super when you stopped working, you may still be able to claim against that policy now, even if you've since rolled your super into another fund.

How do I find out if I had TPD cover?

Start by checking every super fund you've belonged to. You can usually see your super accounts, including old or lost ones, through myGov and the Australian Taxation Office. Then ask each fund for its insurance details and the policy terms that applied when you stopped work.

Here's a simple process:

  1. Log in to myGov and link the ATO to see your super accounts.
  2. Look for old or inactive funds. People often have several from past jobs.
  3. Contact each fund and ask whether you had TPD cover, and from when to when.
  4. Ask for the policy document that applied at the date you stopped work.
  5. Keep everything in writing.

Don't close or roll over old super accounts without checking first. In some cases, moving your super can end the insurance attached to that account. If you've already done this, you may still have a claim based on your earlier cover. Get advice before making further changes.

How long do I have to make a TPD claim?

Time limits for TPD claims vary. Some policies don't set a fixed deadline, while others require notice within a certain period. Separate time limits can also apply to disputes and complaints. Because the rules depend on your fund and policy, it's important to check yours as soon as possible.

Two points are worth knowing:

  • Your policy may say how soon you need to notify the insurer after you stop work. Check the policy terms.
  • If a claim is declined, there are time limits for complaints to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) and for court action. Don't sit on a rejection letter.

Even if you think a lot of time has passed, it's still worth asking. Many people with genuine claims assume they've missed their chance when they haven't.

What evidence will I need?

TPD claims are evidence-heavy. You'll usually need medical reports from your treating doctors and specialists, details of your work history, education and training, and information about when and why you stopped working. The insurer will usually send you forms, and possibly arrange its own medical assessments.

Typical things the insurer asks for:

  • claim forms for you, your treating doctor and sometimes your last employer
  • medical records and specialist reports
  • a summary of your employment history and qualifications
  • payslips or tax records showing when you stopped work
  • any WorkCover or CTP claim documents, if relevant

Your treating doctor's opinion about your ability to work in the future matters a lot. It helps if they understand the specific TPD definition in your policy.

Can I claim TPD as well as WorkCover or CTP?

Often, yes. A TPD claim through super is separate from a WorkCover or motor accident claim. Receiving compensation from one usually doesn't stop you from making the other, although some policies have terms that may interact. Your other claims may also need to consider a TPD payment.

This is an area where advice really helps. The order and timing of different claims can matter, and you want to make sure one claim doesn't accidentally undermine another.

What if my TPD claim is rejected?

A rejection isn't necessarily the final word. You can usually ask your super fund to review the decision through its internal complaints process. If you're still not satisfied, you may be able to complain to AFCA, a free, independent dispute body. In some cases, court action is also an option.

Common reasons for a TPD claim being declined include:

  • the insurer says you could still do some type of work
  • arguments that you weren't covered at the relevant time
  • a pre-existing condition exclusion
  • not enough medical evidence

Insurers must handle claims fairly and in good faith. If a decision seems wrong, it's worth getting a second opinion before accepting it.

Do I need a lawyer for a TPD claim?

You don't have to use one, but many people find it helps. TPD claims involve long forms, detailed medical evidence and policy wording that can be hard to follow. A lawyer can track down your old policies, work with your doctors to address the right definition and deal with the insurer for you.

Will I pay tax on a TPD payout?

There may be tax. TPD benefits paid from super can be taxed, depending on your age, how your super is made up and how the payment is taken. The rules are set by Commonwealth law. Talk to your fund or a registered tax adviser about your situation before making decisions.

Tax shouldn't stop you claiming, but it's worth understanding before you decide what to do with any payment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I make a TPD claim years after I stopped working?

Often, yes. Many TPD policies look at whether you were covered when you stopped work because of your condition, rather than whether you have cover today. Some policies have notice requirements, and time limits apply to disputes, so check your policy and get advice as soon as you can.

I rolled over my super. Have I lost my TPD cover?

Not necessarily for a past disability. If you were covered by your old fund when you stopped work, you may still be able to claim against that fund's policy. Rolling over can end future cover on the old account, so check before making more changes to your super.

Can I claim TPD for a mental health condition?

Yes, TPD policies can cover psychological conditions like depression, anxiety and PTSD, as well as physical conditions. You'll need medical evidence showing that your condition is likely to stop you working permanently in a job suited to your background. The policy definition will decide how this is assessed.

Do I need to have been injured at work to claim TPD?

No. TPD cover through super generally doesn't depend on how or where you got sick or injured. It can apply to illness, accidents outside work, or a condition that developed over time. What matters is whether you meet the policy's definition of TPD.

How long does a TPD claim take?

It varies by insurer and claim. Gathering medical evidence and records usually takes a few months, and the insurer then needs time to assess the claim. Rules require insurers to make decisions within reasonable timeframes. A complete, well-prepared claim can help avoid unnecessary delays.
