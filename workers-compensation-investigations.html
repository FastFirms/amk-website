Workers' compensation claims provide vital support to injured employees, covering lost wages, medical expenses and rehabilitation costs. In some cases, however, the insurer starts a workers' compensation investigation to check that the claim is legitimate.

These investigations are a normal part of the claims process. They help insurers, employers and legal representatives work out liability and identify any potential fraud. This guide explains what happens during a workers' compensation investigation, what investigators look for, and why getting legal advice matters throughout the process.

What are workers' compensation investigations?

Workers' compensation investigations are carried out to check whether a claim is legitimate. When an employee lodges a claim after an injury, the insurer may begin an investigation into the facts. The aim is to gather evidence on whether the injury happened as described, whether the employer was at fault, and whether the worker's employment history supports the claim.

Who is involved in a workers' compensation investigation?

The main parties are the investigator, the injured worker, the employer and any witnesses. Sometimes private investigators are also engaged.

  • Workers' compensation investigator: the main person running the investigation. They may work directly for the insurer or be an external investigator.
  • Private investigators: sometimes external contractors are hired to gather extra evidence or carry out surveillance of the injured worker's activities.
  • Claimant: the employee who lodged the claim.
  • Employer: the business or organisation that may be liable for the injury.
  • Witnesses: people who saw the incident, such as co-workers, who can give a statement.

What does the investigation process involve?

The investigation starts once a claim is lodged. The investigator interviews people, collects statements and gathers evidence. Here is a general breakdown of the process.

1. Initial assessment

The investigator reviews the claim and gathers the basic facts, such as the date, time and place of the injury, along with any documents lodged with the claim, like medical reports or incident reports. They also assess whether the worker's statements line up with the facts.

2. Evidence gathering

Collecting evidence is one of the key parts of the investigation. This can include:

  • Witness statements: speaking with co-workers, supervisors or anyone who saw the incident.
  • Medical records: reviewing the worker's medical history and records to assess the extent of the injury.
  • Employment records: checking the worker's employment history and whether the claim is consistent with their job duties.
  • Surveillance: in some cases, watching the injured worker to see whether their activities contradict their injury claim.

3. Factual investigations and interviews

Investigators carry out factual investigations to check the worker's version of events. This can include:

  • Interviewing the injured worker: the worker is asked about the incident, their injury and their current condition.
  • Interviewing witnesses: witnesses are asked to confirm or contradict the worker's version of events.
  • Reviewing statements: the investigator reviews every statement made by the worker, the employer and witnesses.

4. Employment history review

The investigator reviews the worker's employment history. This helps them decide whether the worker's role fits the nature of the injury and whether there are inconsistencies in the information provided.

5. Fraud detection

Investigators also look for possible fraud. If a worker has exaggerated or made up an injury, or is doing things that contradict their claim, the investigator reports it. Fraudulent claims can lead to serious consequences, including criminal charges or the claim being denied.

Why is legal advice important during an investigation?

Legal advice helps you understand your rights and how to respond. While investigators gather facts and evidence, both injured workers and employers should consider getting legal help to navigate the process and make sure their rights are protected.

For injured workers: getting advice early helps you understand your rights, how to handle the investigation, and what to do if your claim is disputed.

For employers: advice helps you understand your responsibilities and avoid unnecessary liability, and how to take part in the investigation while protecting your interests.

Can I have a support person during an investigation?

Yes. Both workers and employers may have a support person. This can be a union representative, a colleague or a legal adviser who comes with you to interviews or discussions. Their role is to give you support and help with communication during the investigation.

What happens after the investigation?

The investigator prepares a report for the insurer. The report sets out the investigation process, the evidence collected, and the findings and recommendations about liability and whether the claim is valid.

The insurer uses the report to decide whether to accept or reject the claim. The investigator may also prepare a written statement of the findings for any legal proceedings that follow.

An example of how an investigation works

Say a worker claims they hurt their back lifting heavy equipment at work. The investigator may:

  • interview the worker to confirm the details of the injury
  • interview witnesses to check whether the worker was doing the task described when they were hurt
  • review medical records to assess how serious the injury is
  • carry out surveillance, if needed, to see whether the worker is doing things that suggest the injury is less serious than claimed

If the investigator finds the injury is genuine and consistent with the worker's duties, the claim may be accepted. If discrepancies or fraud are found, the claim could be rejected or referred for further legal action.

What is workers' compensation fraud?

Workers' compensation fraud is knowingly giving false information to get benefits you aren't entitled to. It can have serious legal, financial and professional consequences. It can involve:

  • Exaggerating injuries: claiming an injury is more serious than it is.
  • Faking injuries: making up an injury entirely to collect benefits.
  • Not reporting earlier injuries: failing to disclose pre-existing conditions or injuries that could affect the claim.
  • Misrepresenting work capacity: claiming to be unable to work when you can do your duties.

Fraud can be committed by injured workers, medical professionals or employers who misrepresent the facts.

How can I protect myself during an investigation?

Be honest, be accurate, and get advice early. Some practical steps:

  • Injured workers should give honest, accurate details of the injury. Legal advice can help you follow the right procedures and avoid accidentally misrepresenting anything.
  • Employers should keep strong workplace safety systems, review claims regularly, and train supervisors to recognise warning signs of fraud.
  • Medical professionals should document injuries thoroughly and follow proper diagnostic procedures.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my workers' compensation claim being investigated?

Insurers can investigate any claim to check the facts, such as how and where the injury happened and whether it fits your job duties. An investigation doesn't mean you're suspected of fraud. It can be part of the normal assessment of a claim.

Can a workers' compensation investigator follow or film me?

In some cases, yes. Insurers sometimes use surveillance to check whether a worker's activities match their injury claim. Being honest and accurate about your injury and what you can and can't do is the best protection.

Do I have to attend an interview with an investigator?

Investigators usually ask the injured worker for an interview about the incident, the injury and their current condition. You can bring a support person, such as a union representative, colleague or legal adviser. Getting legal advice before the interview can help you understand your rights.

What do workers' compensation investigators look for?

They check whether the injury happened as described, whether it fits your job duties, what witnesses saw, what your medical and employment records show, and whether anything you're doing contradicts your claim.

What happens if an investigation finds fraud?

If an investigator finds an injury was exaggerated or made up, they report it to the insurer. The claim can be rejected, and fraud can lead to criminal charges.
