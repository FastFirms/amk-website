NSW Car Accident Claims: Every Deadline You Need To Know.

Time limits under the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 are strict. Miss the wrong one and you can lose your entitlement to compensation entirely — regardless of how serious your injuries are.

NSW car accident compensation claims are governed by strict time limits under the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 (MAIA) and the Limitation Act 1969 (NSW). These deadlines are not soft guidelines — missing them can permanently extinguish your right to compensation. Here is every deadline you need to know, and what happens if you miss one.

The Key Deadlines

  • 28 days — Notify your CTP insurer as soon as practicable (ideally within 28 days)
  • 3 months — Lodge formal claim form from date of accident or first treatment
  • 3 years — Commence court proceedings for common law damages

1. Notify Your CTP Insurer: As Soon as Practicable (Ideally 28 Days)

As soon as you are injured in a car accident in NSW, you should notify the at-fault driver's CTP insurer. MAIA does not set a single hard notification cutoff, but 28 days is the benchmark — and there are specific 28-day deadlines in certain circumstances (see Nominal Defendant below).

Why notify early:

  • The insurer is required to begin funding your treatment and rehabilitation from notification
  • It creates a contemporaneous record of your injury and the accident — critical if liability is disputed later
  • Delayed notification gives insurers grounds to dispute the causal link between the accident and your injuries
  • Some treatment funding entitlements are triggered by the notification date

If you don't know the insurer's identity, use SIRA's online CTP check tool at sira.nsw.gov.au to find the insurer from the rego number of the at-fault vehicle.

2. Lodge Your Formal Claim: 3 Months

Under MAIA, you must lodge a Personal Injury Claim Form within:

  • 3 months of the date of the accident, or
  • 3 months after you first received treatment for the injury — whichever is later

If you miss this 3-month window, you may lose entitlement to some early statutory benefits, including retrospective income replacement. The insurer may also raise the late lodgement as a ground to limit what they pay.

Extensions are possible in genuine cases — for example, if you were hospitalised and unable to lodge, or if you were unaware of the extent of your injuries — but you cannot rely on an extension. Lodge within 3 months.

3. Commence Court Proceedings: 3 Years

For common law damages — past and future economic loss, care, and general damages (pain and suffering) — you must commence proceedings within 3 years of the date of the accident under the Limitation Act 1969 (NSW).

This is a hard deadline. Unlike some other areas of law, courts have very limited discretion to extend it. If you miss the 3-year limitation period, you will almost certainly lose your right to common law compensation entirely — regardless of how serious your injuries are or how strong your case may have been.

Three years sounds like a long time. In practice, it passes quickly — particularly if you are still recovering, attending medical appointments, and dealing with the impact of your injuries on your work and family. The process of gathering evidence, obtaining independent medical reports, having your injury assessed for Whole Person Impairment, and negotiating with the insurer takes months. Get legal advice well before the 3-year mark.

4. Nominal Defendant Deadlines: Stricter Rules Apply

The Nominal Defendant is a government body that handles CTP claims where the responsible vehicle is unregistered or where the at-fault driver cannot be identified (such as a hit-and-run). The time limits for Nominal Defendant claims are considerably stricter:

  • Unregistered vehicle: You must give notice to the Nominal Defendant within 28 days of the accident
  • Unidentified driver (hit-and-run): You must give notice within 3 months of the accident

These are genuine hard deadlines. If you are injured in a hit-and-run accident and fail to notify the Nominal Defendant within 3 months, your claim may be extinguished. Courts can extend these limits only in exceptional circumstances, and the test is demanding.

If you were involved in a hit-and-run, contact a lawyer immediately — do not wait.

5. Pre-December 2017 Accidents: Different Rules Apply

If your accident occurred before 1 December 2017, your claim is governed by the older Motor Accidents Compensation Act 1999 (MACA), not MAIA. Different rules, different deadlines, and different entitlements apply.

Under MACA, there was a 6-month exemption claim period and different processes for medical assessment and dispute resolution. If your accident predates 1 December 2017, you should obtain specific legal advice about the rules that apply to your claim rather than relying on general information about MAIA.

6. When Can You Get an Extension?

Courts and SIRA have limited discretion to extend time limits. The test is not easy to satisfy, but extensions have been granted where:

  • The claimant was a minor at the time of the accident (the 3-year limitation period generally begins to run from the date they turn 18)
  • The claimant suffered a disability that prevented them from lodging within the standard period
  • The claimant had no reasonable means of discovering the injury was related to the accident within the limitation period (latent injury)
  • There were exceptional circumstances beyond the claimant's control

Extensions are the exception, not the rule. Do not approach deadlines with the assumption that an extension will be available. If you are close to a time limit, contact a lawyer now.
