Minor or Non-Minor? How NSW Classifies Your Road Accident Injury.

In NSW, the single most important determination in your CTP claim is whether your injury is classified as minor or non-minor. It controls how long you receive benefits — and whether you can pursue full compensation at common law.

Under the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 (MAIA), every injury sustained in a NSW road accident is classified as either minor or non-minor. This single classification determines how long you receive statutory benefits, whether you can pursue common law damages, and ultimately how much compensation you may be entitled to. Getting this right matters.

1. Why the Classification Matters

The practical consequences of the minor vs non-minor classification are significant:

  • Minor injury: Statutory benefits — including treatment funding and 85% income replacement — are available for a maximum of 26 weeks from the accident date. There is no entitlement to common law damages.
  • Non-minor injury: Statutory benefits may continue beyond 26 weeks while your claim is resolved. You are eligible to pursue common law damages including full economic loss, care costs, and general damages (pain and suffering) — subject to the 10% Whole Person Impairment threshold for general damages.

The difference between these two pathways can be the difference between a few thousand dollars in benefits and hundreds of thousands in full compensation. The classification is not just administrative — it determines the entire scope of your claim.

2. What Is a Minor Injury Under MAIA?

MAIA defines a minor injury as either:

  • A soft tissue injury — any sprain, strain, or whiplash-type injury affecting muscles, tendons, or ligaments
  • A minor psychological injury

Soft tissue injuries are the most common outcome of road accidents — and the most commonly disputed. A straightforward whiplash injury, a strained neck muscle, or a sprained ankle from a low-speed collision would typically be classified minor. These injuries respond to treatment in most cases and are expected to resolve within the 26-week benefit window.

A minor psychological injury is one that does not substantially impair the person's ability to carry out their usual activities — employment, social life, domestic activities. If the psychological impact is significant and persistent, it may cross into non-minor territory.

3. What Is NOT a Minor Injury

The following injuries fall outside the minor injury definition under MAIA and are therefore non-minor:

  • Fractures — including hairline fractures, stress fractures, and small chip fractures. Any fracture is non-minor.
  • Structural damage to the spine — disc prolapse, disc herniation, disc bulge with nerve root compression, damage to the vertebral endplates
  • Nerve damage — peripheral nerve injuries, radiculopathy (nerve root compression causing radiating pain, numbness, or weakness)
  • Lacerations requiring surgery
  • Burns
  • Serious psychological injury — PTSD, major depressive disorder, or adjustment disorder that substantially impairs the person's ability to work, maintain relationships, or carry out daily activities

Common Injuries That Are NOT Minor Despite Appearing So

Some injuries that may seem relatively minor at first present as non-minor once fully investigated:

  • Disc prolapse with nerve symptoms — radiating arm or leg pain, numbness, or weakness following a whiplash-type mechanism. What initially appears to be soft tissue neck pain may involve a prolapsed disc compressing a nerve root. This is non-minor.
  • Fractured ribs — a common injury in side-impact collisions that is sometimes overlooked in initial assessments. Any rib fracture is non-minor.
  • PTSD after a serious accident — anxiety, hypervigilance, intrusive memories, and avoidance behaviours that significantly impair daily function constitute a serious psychological injury and are non-minor.

If you have any of these symptoms, do not accept an initial minor classification without seeking a second opinion.

4. How the Classification Is Made

The classification process works as follows:

  1. Initial classification by the insurer: Based on medical certificates, hospital records, GP notes, and any early specialist reports, the CTP insurer makes an initial classification of your injury as minor or non-minor.
  2. Dispute: If you disagree with the insurer's classification — or the insurer disputes a treating doctor's classification — either party can refer the dispute to SIRA's dispute resolution service.
  3. Independent Medical Assessment: An independent medical examiner assesses the nature of your injury and determines whether it meets the minor injury definition under MAIA. This assessment can be challenged further if necessary.

The key point: the insurer's initial classification is not final. If you have been told your injury is minor but you have symptoms consistent with a non-minor injury — nerve pain, persistent weakness, significant psychological impact — you have the right to dispute that classification.

5. Why Insurers Dispute Classifications

CTP insurers have a direct financial interest in classifying injuries as minor. If your injury is minor, their maximum liability is 26 weeks of statutory benefits — treatment and income replacement. If your injury is non-minor, their exposure extends to potentially years of benefits and, ultimately, a common law damages claim that could be worth substantially more.

This means insurers scrutinise early medical records closely and may dispute a non-minor classification where they believe the evidence supports a soft tissue injury only. Do not assume the insurer's classification is correct or in your interests. If your symptoms are consistent with a non-minor injury, get legal advice and, if appropriate, independent specialist assessment before accepting the classification.

6. The 10% WPI Hurdle for General Damages

Even if your injury is classified non-minor, there is a further threshold for the general damages component of a common law claim — pain, suffering, and loss of enjoyment of life.

To claim general damages, your injury must be assessed at 10% or more Whole Person Impairment (WPI) by an accredited medical assessor. WPI is assessed using standardised guidelines, and the assessment considers the permanent impact of your injuries across all body systems.

If your WPI is below 10%, you cannot claim general damages — but you can still claim economic loss (past and future wage loss, reduced earning capacity) and care costs, with no WPI threshold applying to those heads of damage.

This means that even an injury assessed below the 10% WPI threshold can support a meaningful common law claim if you suffered significant wage loss or incurred ongoing care and treatment costs.
