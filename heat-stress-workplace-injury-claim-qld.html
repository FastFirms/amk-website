Suffered Heat Stroke or Heat Illness at Work in Queensland? You May Have a Claim.

Each Queensland summer, workers collapse on construction sites, in cane fields, on delivery routes, and in warehouses that should never have been as hot as they were. Heat stroke and heat exhaustion are not freak accidents — they are the predictable result of employers who fail to manage a hazard they are legally required to control. If you or someone you know has suffered a heat illness at work in Queensland, the law gives you more options than most workers realise.

Spring is the right time to understand those options. The dangerous heat is coming, and if you're already dealing with the aftermath of a heat illness from last summer, time limits are running.

Is heat illness a workplace injury under Queensland law?

It can be. Under section 32 of the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003 (Qld), an "injury" is a personal injury arising out of, or in the course of, employment if the employment is a significant contributing factor to the injury.

Heat exhaustion, heat stroke and related conditions can meet that test. A heat illness suffered while doing your normal duties in hot conditions will often do so. But the test is not automatic. Where there are other possible causes, such as a pre-existing heart condition, the insurer may look closely at whether work was a significant contributing factor. Early medical evidence linking your illness to your working conditions is important.

If your injury is accepted, you can receive statutory WorkCover benefits. Depending on your circumstances, you may also be able to claim common law damages.

What employers must do about heat

Under the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Qld), a person conducting a business or undertaking must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers. Heat is one of the risks they must manage.

Queensland law does not set a temperature at which work must stop, and there is no exposure standard for heat stress. Instead, employers must assess the risk, taking into account air temperature, humidity, air flow, radiant heat, how physical the work is, clothing and PPE, and the individual worker, and then put suitable controls in place.

Workplace Health and Safety Queensland guidance and the Managing the work environment and facilities Code of Practice 2021 (as varied from 1 September 2026) describe controls such as:

  • Rest breaks in shade or cool areas, which become more frequent and longer as heat and humidity rise
  • Access to cool drinking water close to where people are working, with workers encouraged to drink regularly
  • Acclimatisation for workers who are new to hot conditions or returning from leave, for example by gradually building up their exposure
  • Monitoring conditions at the worksite and changing work patterns, such as rescheduling heavy tasks to cooler parts of the day
  • Training and supervision, so workers can recognise heat illness in themselves and others and know how to respond
  • First aid and emergency plans that are suitable for heat risks

These are not fixed legal minimums. If an employer failed to take reasonably practicable steps like these, that failure can be important evidence in a negligence claim.

Industries Most at Risk in Queensland
  • Construction — outdoor work on Brisbane 2032 projects, roofing, civil earthworks
  • Agriculture — cane harvesting, fruit picking, horticulture across Far North QLD and the Darling Downs
  • Transport and logistics — delivery drivers in poorly ventilated vehicles, warehouse workers in non-air-conditioned facilities
  • Mining and resources — surface operations in central and north-west Queensland
  • Seasonal and working holiday workers — who may be unfamiliar with Queensland heat and lack the bargaining power to raise safety concerns

WorkCover QLD — your immediate no-fault entitlement

WorkCover is your first port of call. You do not need to prove your employer was negligent — you simply need to establish that you suffered a heat illness in the course of your employment. Lodge your claim within six months of the date of illness.

Statutory benefits cover medical treatment, income replacement while you are unable to work, and a lump sum for any permanent impairment. Heat stroke that causes lasting organ damage — cardiac injury, kidney damage, neurological effects — can produce significant permanent impairment assessments. Make sure your treating doctors document every ongoing consequence of the heat illness, not just the acute presentation.

See a doctor on the day of the incident, or as soon as you are physically able. The contemporaneous medical record is the foundation of your claim. Do not understate your symptoms in that first consultation.

Common law damages

If your employer's negligence caused your heat illness, you may be able to claim common law damages as well as statutory benefits. These damages can include past and future loss of income, medical and care costs, and general damages for pain and suffering.

In Queensland, workers may bring a common law claim whatever their level of permanent impairment. If your degree of permanent impairment is assessed at less than 5%, however, you may have to choose between accepting a statutory lump sum and pursuing common law damages. That choice can have major financial consequences, so get legal advice before you accept or reject any lump sum offer.

A common law claim is based on negligence. The WHS Act does not itself give you a right to sue, but your employer's WHS duties and the relevant guidance help show what a reasonable employer should have done. Claims must also follow the pre-court procedures in Chapter 5 of the Act and meet time limits.

Evidence that can support a negligence claim includes:

  • No shade or inadequate shade was provided
  • Water was not available or not encouraged
  • No breaks were provided or breaks were unreasonably short
  • The worker was new or recently returned and no acclimatisation was provided
  • Work continuing unchanged despite known extreme conditions
  • Earlier heat incidents that were reported but not acted on

Every case depends on its facts. Your employer may dispute what caused your illness or argue that you contributed to it.

Seasonal workers and working holiday visa holders

Queensland's agricultural sector employs large numbers of seasonal workers and backpackers on working holiday visas. These workers are entitled to the same WorkCover protections as any other Queensland worker — visa status does not affect WorkCover eligibility. They are also among the most vulnerable to heat illness, having arrived from cooler climates and often lacking the experience or confidence to raise safety concerns with employers.

If you are a seasonal or working holiday worker who has suffered heat illness on a Queensland farm or in a packing shed, you have rights. Language barriers do not prevent a claim, and AMK Compensation Lawyers can assist in obtaining translation support.

Steps to take if you or a colleague suffers heat illness at work

  1. Call 000 for severe symptoms — confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures, and stopping sweating in a hot environment are medical emergencies. Heat stroke kills. Do not wait.
  2. Move to a cool area and apply cooling — cool water, ice packs to neck, armpits, and groin. Fan the person. These are immediate first aid measures while waiting for emergency services.
  3. Report to your employer in writing — as soon as practicable. Email or text creates a record. Note the time, the temperature conditions, what breaks had been provided, and what water was available.
  4. See a doctor the same day — even if you feel recovered. Heat illness can cause internal damage that only becomes apparent after the fact. A same-day medical record is essential.
  5. Document conditions — note the ambient temperature, whether shade was available, when you last had a break, and how much water you had consumed. A contemporaneous note is far more compelling than recollection months later.
  6. Contact a lawyer before giving statements to WorkCover or your employer — particularly if the injury is serious. You are entitled to independent legal advice at any stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is heat stroke or heat exhaustion at work a compensable injury in Queensland?

It can be, if it arose out of or in the course of your employment and your employment was a significant contributing factor. Heat stroke, heat exhaustion, heat syncope and heat cramps can all qualify. WorkCover is no-fault, so you do not need to prove negligence to receive statutory benefits.

Can I sue my employer as well as claiming WorkCover?

Possibly, if your employer was negligent and that negligence caused your injury. There is no minimum impairment level to bring a common law claim. If your impairment is assessed at less than 5%, you may have to choose between a statutory lump sum and common law damages. Get advice before you decide.

Is there a temperature at which work must stop in Queensland?

No. There is no fixed temperature limit. Employers must manage heat risks as far as is reasonably practicable, taking into account all the conditions and the work being done.

What should I do if I or a colleague develops heat illness at work?

Call 000 for serious symptoms such as confusion, collapse, seizures or loss of consciousness — heat stroke is a medical emergency. Move the person somewhere cool and start cooling them using cool water, fanning and cold packs while waiting for help. Report the incident to your employer in writing as soon as you can. See a doctor the same day, even if you feel better. Write down the conditions: the temperature, the shade, your breaks, the water available and the tasks you were doing. Get legal advice before giving detailed statements or making decisions about lump sum offers.
