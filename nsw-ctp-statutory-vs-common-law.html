NSW CTP Claims: Statutory Benefits vs Common Law Explained.

NSW operates a two-tier CTP system under the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017. Understanding which tier applies to you — and when — is the difference between a partial recovery and full compensation.

When you're injured in a car accident in NSW, the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 (MAIA) provides two separate pathways for compensation. Many people receive statutory benefits in the early weeks without realising there may be a second, larger claim available to them. Others assume their soft tissue injury rules out any further recovery. Both assumptions can cost you.

The MAIA Two-Tier System

MAIA replaced the old Motor Accidents Compensation Act 1999 for accidents occurring from 1 December 2017. The new scheme introduced a structured two-tier approach:

  1. Tier 1 — Statutory benefits: No-fault, available to everyone injured in a road accident in NSW
  2. Tier 2 — Common law damages: Fault-based, available only to those with non-minor injuries who can establish the other driver's negligence

These two pathways are not mutually exclusive. You can receive statutory benefits while your common law claim is being resolved.

Tier 1: Statutory Benefits

Who receives them

Statutory benefits are available to all injured road users — drivers, passengers, cyclists, and pedestrians — regardless of who caused the accident. Even if you were partially or entirely at fault, you are entitled to statutory benefits.

What statutory benefits cover

  • Medical and hospital treatment: Reasonable and necessary treatment related to your injury
  • Rehabilitation: Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychological treatment
  • Income replacement: 85% of your pre-accident weekly earnings, capped at approximately $7,500 per week (indexed annually)

How long do statutory benefits last?

This is where the distinction between minor and non-minor injuries becomes critical:

  • Minor injury: Statutory benefits are available for up to 26 weeks from the date of the accident
  • Non-minor injury: Benefits may continue for longer while your claim is being resolved

The Minor vs Non-Minor Distinction

This classification is the most consequential determination in any NSW CTP claim. It affects both the duration of your statutory benefits and your eligibility for common law damages.

Minor injury

Under MAIA, a minor injury is defined as:

  • A soft tissue injury — sprains, strains, or whiplash-type injury to muscles, tendons, or ligaments
  • A minor psychological injury

These are the most common injuries from road accidents. They heal with treatment in most cases and are subject to the 26-week benefit cap.

Non-minor injury

Injuries that fall outside the minor injury definition include:

  • Fractures (including small or hairline fractures)
  • Structural damage to the spine — disc prolapse, disc bulge with nerve root compression
  • Nerve damage
  • Lacerations requiring surgery
  • Burns
  • Serious psychological injury — PTSD, adjustment disorder that substantially impairs function

If your injury is non-minor, your statutory benefits may continue beyond 26 weeks while your claim progresses, and you may be eligible to pursue common law damages.

Tier 2: Common Law Damages

Who can pursue common law damages

To access common law damages under MAIA, you must satisfy three conditions:

  1. Your injury is not minor
  2. You can establish that the at-fault driver's negligence caused or contributed to your injury
  3. For general damages (pain and suffering), you meet the 10% Whole Person Impairment (WPI) threshold

What common law damages cover

Common law damages can include:

  • Past economic loss — wages or income you have already lost
  • Future economic loss — reduced earning capacity going forward
  • Past and future medical expenses
  • Care and assistance — both paid care and care provided by family members
  • General damages — pain, suffering, and loss of enjoyment of life (subject to the 10% WPI threshold)

The 10% WPI threshold for general damages

Whole Person Impairment is assessed by an independent medical examiner using the NSW Workers Compensation Guidelines. If your assessed WPI is below 10%, you cannot claim general damages — but you can still claim economic loss and other heads of damage.

This threshold applies only to the general damages component. Many claimants with assessments just below 10% still recover substantial compensation through economic loss and care claims.

No WPI threshold for economic loss

This is a point many claimants and even some practitioners miss: there is no WPI threshold for economic loss claims. If you can establish that the at-fault driver caused your injury and you suffered wage loss or reduced earning capacity as a result, you can pursue economic loss damages regardless of your WPI assessment.

Can I Claim Both Statutory Benefits and Common Law?

Yes. Statutory benefits are entirely separate from common law damages. You receive statutory benefits — treatment funding and income replacement — throughout the claim process. Once your injuries have stabilised and your prognosis is clear, your common law claim is assessed separately. One does not preclude the other.

However, any amounts paid under statutory benefits may be taken into account when calculating your common law settlement, to avoid double recovery for the same loss.

Commencing Your Claim

Common law proceedings must be commenced within 3 years of the date of accident (Limitation Act 1969 NSW). This is a hard deadline. If you are considering a common law claim, do not wait — the process of gathering evidence, obtaining medical reports, and negotiating with the insurer takes time.

Getting early legal advice ensures you understand which pathway applies, whether your injury is correctly classified, and that you don't miss critical deadlines or accept a settlement that undervalues your long-term losses.
