What To Do After A Car Accident In NSW.

NSW is different to other states. Your CTP claim is governed by the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 (MAIA) and funded by your green slip. The steps you take in the first hours and weeks shape everything that follows.

If you've been involved in a car accident in NSW, the CTP (Compulsory Third Party) scheme applies — this is the green slip insurance every registered vehicle must carry. Under the Motor Accidents Injuries Act 2017 (MAIA), you're entitled to statutory benefits regardless of fault, and potentially common law compensation if your injury is serious. Here's what to do, step by step.

1. Get Safe and Call 000 If Anyone Is Injured

Your immediate priority is safety. Move vehicles off the road if it's safe to do so and turn on hazard lights. Call 000 immediately if anyone is injured, unconscious, or trapped. Do not move injured people unless they are in immediate danger — you could worsen spinal injuries.

While you wait for emergency services, check on other parties involved, note witness details, and take photos of the scene, vehicle positions, damage, and any road conditions.

2. Exchange Details

You are legally required to exchange the following information with other drivers involved:

  • Full name and address
  • Driver's licence number
  • Vehicle registration number
  • CTP insurer — this is shown on the green slip, which is often found in the glovebox or attached to the vehicle's registration papers

If you can't find the other driver's CTP insurer on the day, you can look it up later using SIRA's online CTP check tool (search "SIRA CTP check" at sira.nsw.gov.au) using the vehicle's rego number.

How to Find the CTP Insurer

  • Check the rego papers: The green slip certificate identifies the CTP insurer.
  • Check the green slip itself: If the other party has it in the vehicle.
  • Use SIRA's online tool: Visit sira.nsw.gov.au and search by rego number — you'll be directed to the correct insurer.
  • Nominal Defendant: If the vehicle was unregistered or the driver fled the scene, contact the Nominal Defendant (a government body that handles these claims).

3. Report to Police

In NSW, you must report an accident to police if:

  • Anyone was injured
  • A vehicle cannot be driven away from the scene
  • There is a dispute about liability or the other driver fails to stop
  • The other driver appeared to be under the influence of alcohol or drugs

Even if none of these apply, it's worth making a police report if there are any injuries — it creates an independent contemporaneous record of what happened.

4. See a Doctor as Soon as Possible

See a GP or attend an emergency department even if you feel minor pain. This is important for two reasons: your health, and your claim.

Under MAIA, your entitlement to statutory benefits — including funding for treatment and income replacement — is linked to your medical treatment. A medical certificate completed by your treating doctor is required to activate statutory benefits. The earlier you're assessed, the stronger your claim record.

Many injuries, including whiplash, soft tissue damage, and concussion, are not immediately apparent. Delayed treatment weakens your medical evidence and gives insurers grounds to dispute causation.

5. Notify Your CTP Insurer

Under MAIA, you should notify the at-fault driver's CTP insurer as soon as practicable — ideally within 28 days of the accident. This is not a hard legal cutoff for all claims, but notifying early:

  • Triggers the insurer's obligation to pay for treatment and rehabilitation
  • Creates a formal record of when you first reported the injury
  • Avoids disputes later about why you delayed

Call the insurer directly using the number on the green slip, or find them through SIRA's website. The State Insurance Regulatory Authority (SIRA) regulates the NSW CTP scheme — their website is a useful resource throughout your claim.

6. Lodge Your Claim Form

There are two stages to notifying the insurer and lodging a formal claim:

  • Accident Notification Form (ANF): A brief initial notification that can be lodged at any stage.
  • Personal Injury Claim Form: The full claim form, which must be lodged within 3 months of the accident or 3 months after you first received treatment, whichever is later.

If you miss the 3-month deadline, you may lose entitlement to some early benefits, though extensions are available in genuine cases. Do not leave this until the last minute.

7. Be Careful with Early Offers

In the early weeks, insurers may offer to fund treatment under statutory benefits — this is appropriate and expected. However, be cautious about any suggestion that you should settle your entire claim early.

Under MAIA, there are two separate pathways:

  • Statutory benefits: Available to all injured road users, regardless of fault. Covers medical treatment and income replacement. These can begin immediately.
  • Common law damages: Available only for non-minor injuries where the at-fault driver's negligence can be proven. This is a separate, larger claim that should only be settled once your injuries have stabilised and your prognosis is clear.

Settling your statutory benefits entitlement does not prevent you from pursuing common law damages — but accepting a combined settlement before you fully understand your long-term prognosis can significantly undervalue your claim.
