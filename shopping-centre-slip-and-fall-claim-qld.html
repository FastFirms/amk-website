It happens in a second. A spilled drink near the food court, a wet floor with no sign, a broken tile, a cracked car park kerb. One moment you're grabbing groceries, the next you're on the ground and wondering what just happened.

Lots of people pick themselves up, feel embarrassed and just go home. Then the pain kicks in. If you've been hurt in a fall at a shopping centre, supermarket or store anywhere in Queensland, this guide explains your options. As Ipswich personal injury lawyers based in Augustine Heights, we know how a "small" fall can turn into weeks off work.

This information applies to Queensland. The rules in other states are different.

Can I claim if I slipped or tripped at a shopping centre?

You may be able to. In Queensland, a shopping centre owner, manager or store occupier generally has a legal duty to take reasonable care to keep the premises safe for visitors. If they didn't take reasonable care and you were injured because of it, you may be able to make a public liability claim.

"Public liability" just means a claim against someone responsible for a place where you were injured, like a business, landlord or council. These claims are made against that party's insurer in most cases.

The key word is reasonable. Not every fall leads to a successful claim. The question is usually whether the centre or store did what a reasonable operator would do to prevent the hazard. That might include regular floor inspections, prompt clean-ups, warning signs and fixing damaged surfaces.

What should I do straight after the fall?

Look after your health first, then make a record. Get first aid or medical help. If you can, report the incident to centre management or store staff before you leave, and ask for it to be written in their incident register. Take photos of the hazard and note any witnesses.

Here's a quick checklist:

  • Get medical help. See a GP or go to hospital if needed. Tell them you fell and how.
  • Report it on the spot. Ask centre management or store staff to record the incident. Ask for a copy or a reference number.
  • Photograph everything. Take photos of the spill, the uneven surface, the lighting, any missing signs and your shoes.
  • Get witness details. Names and phone numbers of anyone who saw it.
  • Note the time and exact location. "Near the escalator outside the chemist, about 11.15am" is far more useful than "at the shops".
  • Keep what you were wearing. Don't throw out shoes or clothing, as they may be relevant.

Didn't do all of this? Don't panic. Many people don't, especially if they were hurt or shaken. A lawyer can help gather evidence afterwards.

Why does CCTV matter so much?

Most shopping centres have CCTV, but footage isn't kept forever. It may be recorded over within a set period, sometimes quite quickly. Footage can show exactly how you fell, how long the hazard was there and whether anyone checked the area. That can make a big difference to a claim.

If you think you may want to claim, ask centre management in writing to keep the footage of your fall. A lawyer can also send a formal request. The sooner it's asked for, the better the chance it still exists.

Who is responsible — the centre or the store?

It depends on where you fell. In a common area, like a walkway, food court or car park, the centre's owner or manager is often responsible. Inside a shop, the business running that store may be responsible. Sometimes responsibility is shared, including with cleaning or maintenance contractors.

This is one of the trickier parts of a shopping centre claim. Large centres often have:

  • an owner (sometimes a property trust or investment group)
  • a centre management company
  • separate tenants running each shop
  • contractors for cleaning, security and maintenance

You don't need to figure this out alone. Part of a lawyer's job is identifying the right party, or parties, to make the claim against. In the Springfield and Ipswich areas, with major centres such as Orion Springfield Central and Riverlink, that can involve several companies.

How long do I have to claim in Queensland?

There are strict deadlines. In Queensland, public liability claims generally must start with a formal notice to the party you're claiming against. It's usually due within nine months of the incident (or of symptoms first appearing), or within one month of instructing a lawyer once the other party is identified, whichever is earlier. There's also an overall time limit, generally three years from the injury, to start court proceedings. Missing a deadline can put your claim at risk.

Queensland's Personal Injuries Proceedings Act 2002 sets out the pre-court steps for most public liability claims. The deadlines can be shorter than people expect, especially once you've spoken with a lawyer. Check your situation sooner rather than later.

What if I wasn't watching where I was going?

You might still have a claim, but it could be reduced. If the insurer argues you were distracted, like looking at your phone, or that the hazard was obvious, they may say you contributed to your own injury. If that's accepted, your compensation could be reduced. It doesn't automatically mean you have no claim.

Queensland law also says a business doesn't have to warn you about risks that are "obvious" to a reasonable person. Whether a hazard was obvious depends on the facts. A clear puddle in a well-lit walkway is different from a clear liquid on a shiny floor under bright lights.

Be honest about what happened. A lawyer can assess how these arguments might play out before you decide anything.

What can I claim for?

That depends on your injury and its impact on your life. A public liability claim may cover medical and rehabilitation costs, lost wages now and in the future, help you need at home, and an amount for pain and suffering. Each claim is assessed on its own facts, and nobody can tell you in advance what you'll receive.

Queensland law places limits and thresholds on some types of damages in personal injury claims, and some amounts are indexed and change each year. That's another reason to get proper advice about your own claim rather than relying on what happened to someone else.

Common shopping centre fall injuries we hear about include:

  • wrist and arm fractures from trying to break a fall
  • knee and ankle injuries
  • back and neck injuries
  • head injuries, especially for older people

If you're older or have an existing condition, a fall can have a bigger impact than it would on someone younger. That's still relevant to your claim. The law generally takes you as you are.

What if I fell in a shopping centre car park?

Car park falls can be claimed in the same way. Potholes, uneven kerbs, poor lighting and slippery surfaces in a centre car park are usually the responsibility of the centre's owner or manager. If a vehicle was involved, different rules may apply, because the claim may be a motor vehicle claim instead.

If you were hit or knocked over by a car in a car park, the claim may be against that car's CTP insurer, not the centre. A lawyer can work out which pathway applies.

Do I need a lawyer for a slip and fall claim?

You don't have to use one, but it can make the process less stressful. You'll be dealing with large companies and their insurers, formal notices and strict deadlines while you're recovering. A lawyer can manage the paperwork, collect evidence like CCTV and help keep your claim on track.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I claim if I slipped on a wet floor in a supermarket?

You may be able to. In Queensland, a supermarket generally has to take reasonable care to keep its floors safe, such as cleaning spills promptly and using warning signs. If it didn't, and you were hurt as a result, you may have a public liability claim. Whether you can claim depends on the facts, so get advice early.

What if the shopping centre says it's not their fault?

That's common. Centres and their insurers often deny responsibility at first, or point to a tenant or contractor. An initial denial doesn't mean you have no claim. Evidence like CCTV, cleaning records and incident reports can show what really happened. A lawyer can request these documents as part of the claim process.

How long after a fall can I make a claim in Queensland?

There are strict deadlines. A formal notice of claim generally must be given within nine months of the incident, or one month after you instruct a lawyer if that's earlier, and court proceedings generally must start within three years of the injury. The early deadline can come around faster than you'd think, so check your situation as soon as possible.

Do I have to go to court?

Usually not. In Queensland, most public liability claims go through a pre-court process, including information exchange and a compulsory conference where the parties try to settle. Court proceedings are only needed if the claim can't be resolved. Many claims settle without ever reaching a courtroom.

I didn't report the fall at the time. Can I still claim?

Possibly. Not reporting it straight away makes things harder, but it doesn't automatically end your claim. Report it in writing to centre management as soon as you can, see a doctor and write down everything you remember. Witnesses, CCTV and medical records may still support what happened.
