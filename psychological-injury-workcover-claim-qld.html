Psychological injuries at work are rising. WorkCover Queensland data consistently shows mental health claims as one of the fastest-growing injury categories — and also among the most likely to be disputed. If you have developed anxiety, depression, PTSD or another psychological condition because of events at work, this article explains what the law requires, what can block a claim, and what your options are if WorkCover refuses.

Are psychological injuries compensable under WorkCover in Queensland?

Yes. Under the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003 (Qld), a "work-related injury" includes personal injury arising out of, or in the course of, employment. Psychological or psychiatric conditions are personal injuries under that definition. WorkCover Queensland cannot refuse a claim simply because the injury is psychological rather than physical.

Psychological injuries that are regularly accepted as compensable include:

  • Anxiety disorders — generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety triggered by workplace events
  • Depression arising from sustained work stress, bullying or traumatic incidents
  • Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) following workplace trauma such as witnessing a serious accident, assault at work, or first-responder exposure
  • Adjustment disorder arising from significant changes to working conditions or workplace relationships
  • Burnout that has progressed to a diagnosable mental health condition

A diagnosis alone is not sufficient. You also need to meet the threshold test — that employment was a "significant contributing factor" to the injury.

The significant contributing factor test

For a psychological injury to be compensable, your employment must be a significant contributing factor to the injury. This is an important and sometimes misunderstood threshold.

It does not mean employment must be the only cause, or even the main cause. It means employment must be a real and meaningful contributor to the development of the condition — not merely incidental to it. If you have a pre-existing vulnerability to anxiety or depression, that does not automatically defeat your claim, as long as employment is still a significant contributing factor.

WorkCover will look at the totality of your circumstances, including any events outside work that may have contributed to your condition. The assessment is factual, not formulaic — the nature of the workplace stressors, the severity of your symptoms, the timing of their onset, and the medical evidence about causation will all be relevant.

Key distinction

A pre-existing psychological condition does not prevent a successful WorkCover claim. If your pre-existing condition was worsened or aggravated by work events, you may still have a valid claim for the aggravation. The test focuses on the work-related contribution to the injury as it now exists, not on whether the person had a "clean slate" before starting the job.

The management action exclusion — the most common reason claims fail

The management action exclusion is the most significant barrier to psychological injury claims in Queensland. Under section 32 of the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003, a psychological injury is not compensable if it arises wholly or predominantly from one or more of the following actions taken by the employer:

  • Deciding to transfer, promote or demote the worker
  • Taking disciplinary action
  • Taking action because the worker's performance was unsatisfactory
  • Deciding to retrench or dismiss the worker
  • A decision not to renew, or the non-renewal of, a fixed-term contract

Crucially, the exclusion only applies where the action was reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way. This is not a blanket shield for employers. If the management action was itself unreasonable — the decision was disproportionate, procedurally unfair, made in bad faith, or carried out in a way that was humiliating or degrading — the exclusion may not apply.

The question courts and WorkCover decision-makers ask is not simply whether the employer had the power to take the action, but whether the action and the manner in which it was carried out were objectively reasonable. That is a nuanced assessment, and the facts matter enormously.

Common scenarios where the exclusion does and does not apply

Scenario Likely position
Formal performance review conducted fairly with documented concerns Exclusion likely applies
Demotion after legitimate organisational restructure Exclusion likely applies
Repeated public humiliation by a supervisor in front of colleagues Exclusion less likely to apply
Performance management used selectively as a pretext for targeting one employee Exclusion less likely to apply
Disciplinary action with excessive, unfounded allegations designed to intimidate Exclusion less likely to apply
Systemic workplace bullying by co-workers unrelated to any management process Exclusion does not apply
Witnessing a traumatic workplace accident Exclusion does not apply

These scenarios are illustrative only. Each case turns on its own facts, and WorkCover's initial decision is not the final word.

Workplace bullying and psychological injury

Workplace bullying is defined under Queensland's work health and safety laws as repeated unreasonable behaviour directed at a worker or group of workers that creates a risk to health and safety. Where bullying causes a diagnosable psychological injury, that injury can be a compensable WorkCover claim.

The critical word is "repeated." A single incident of unfair treatment will generally not constitute bullying, though it may still be relevant to other causes of action. Bullying typically involves a pattern of conduct — ongoing belittling, unreasonable criticism, exclusion, intimidation, or a hostile working environment that persists over time.

Employers have a duty under the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Qld) to manage the risk of psychological injury in the workplace. Where an employer is on notice of bullying — through formal complaints, HR reports, or obvious signs of distress — and fails to act, that failure may support both a WorkCover claim and a common law negligence claim.

What if WorkCover rejects my psychological injury claim?

A rejection is not the end. WorkCover's initial decision is reviewable. If your claim is rejected, you can:

  1. Apply to WorkCover for an internal review — WorkCover must review its own decision. You have a limited time to request this, so act quickly.
  2. Appeal to the Queensland Industrial Relations Commission (QIRC) — if the internal review does not resolve the matter, you can appeal to the QIRC, which is an independent tribunal. The QIRC will assess the evidence afresh and is not bound by WorkCover's reasoning.
  3. Obtain further medical evidence — in many cases, a rejected psychological injury claim can be successfully challenged with better-documented medical evidence from a treating psychiatrist or psychologist who can clearly address the statutory test.

Many psychological injury claims that are initially rejected succeed on review or appeal when properly prepared. The management action exclusion in particular requires careful factual analysis, and WorkCover's initial assessment of what constitutes "reasonable" management action is not always correct.

Common law claims for psychological injury

A statutory WorkCover claim and a common law damages claim are different pathways. The statutory claim provides income replacement, medical treatment, and potentially a DPI lump sum. The common law claim — which requires establishing employer negligence — can provide substantially greater compensation, including full past and future economic loss, care costs, and general damages for pain and suffering.

For a common law claim based on psychological injury, you need to establish:

  1. Your employer owed you a duty of care (which is generally clear)
  2. Your employer breached that duty — for example, by failing to respond to known workplace bullying, by implementing an unreasonably stressful workload, or by allowing conditions that any reasonable employer would have identified as a mental health risk
  3. The breach caused or materially contributed to your psychological injury
  4. You suffered loss as a result

Psychiatric evidence is central to a common law psychological injury claim. A treating psychiatrist's or psychologist's opinion about causation, prognosis, and the impact of the injury on your ability to work is essential. If your condition is likely to affect your earning capacity in the long term, the economic loss component of a common law claim can be significant.

DPI and your options

Psychological injuries can result in a degree of permanent impairment (DPI) assessment under WorkCover. If your DPI is below 20% (or you have no DPI), accepting the statutory lump sum bars a common law claim — these choices are mutually exclusive at that level. If your DPI is 20% or above, you can receive the impairment lump sum and still pursue common law. Separate physical and psychiatric DPIs cannot be added together to reach 20%. This decision is irreversible. Do not accept a lump sum without understanding the full value of your common law entitlements first.

Practical steps if you have a psychological injury at work

If you are dealing with a psychological injury caused by events at work, these steps matter:

  • See a doctor as soon as possible. A formal diagnosis from a treating GP, psychiatrist or psychologist is the foundation of any claim. The timing of the diagnosis relative to the workplace events is important — delays create credibility issues that WorkCover and defendants will exploit.
  • Document the workplace conduct. Keep a written record of incidents — dates, what was said or done, who was present, and how it made you feel. If you have emails, messages, or other evidence of bullying or unreasonable conduct, preserve them.
  • Make a formal complaint if appropriate. An internal complaint to HR or a report under your employer's workplace health and safety policies creates a contemporaneous record of the problem and of your employer's response (or failure to respond). This can be significant evidence in a later dispute.
  • Lodge a WorkCover claim promptly. Do not wait. The six-month lodgement window applies to psychological injuries just as it does to physical injuries.
  • Get legal advice before the claim is decided. The management action exclusion is a complex area of law. The way a claim is framed and the medical evidence that supports it can determine whether it is accepted or rejected.
  • Do not resign without legal advice. Resigning can affect your workers compensation entitlements and may limit your ability to demonstrate ongoing incapacity. Speak to a lawyer before making that decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you claim WorkCover for a psychological injury in Queensland?

Yes. Psychological injuries are compensable under the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003 (Qld) in the same way as physical injuries. You must show that your employment was a "significant contributing factor" to the injury. Common psychological injuries that are accepted include anxiety disorders, depression, adjustment disorder, and PTSD arising from workplace events such as bullying, harassment, or traumatic incidents.

What is the management action exclusion for WorkCover psychological claims?

Section 32 of the Act excludes psychological injuries that arise wholly or predominantly from reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way — for example, a disciplinary process, a performance review, or a decision to retrench. However, if the management action was unreasonable, was carried out in an unreasonable way, or if the injury was caused by bullying that goes beyond any management action, the exclusion may not apply.

Does WorkCover cover workplace bullying psychological injuries in Queensland?

Yes, in many cases. Systemic bullying or harassment that causes a psychological injury can be compensable under WorkCover. The key distinction is whether the conduct amounts to reasonable management action. Repeated unreasonable conduct that creates a hostile or intimidating environment is generally not protected by the management action exclusion. Each case turns on its own facts.

What is the significant contributing factor test for WorkCover psychological injuries?

For a psychological injury to be compensable under WorkCover in Queensland, employment must be a "significant contributing factor" to the injury. This is a lower threshold than "the main cause" — it means employment must be a real and meaningful contributor to the injury, not merely incidental. Personal factors such as a pre-existing condition can co-exist with a valid WorkCover claim if employment is still a significant contributing factor.

Can you make a common law claim for a psychological injury in Queensland?

Yes, if your psychological injury was caused or materially contributed to by your employer's negligence. Common scenarios include an employer who failed to act on known workplace bullying, failed to implement adequate mental health supports in a high-stress workplace, or whose unreasonable treatment of an employee caused a diagnosable injury. A common law claim allows recovery of past and future economic loss, medical and care expenses, and general damages.
